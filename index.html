Method

These are not brand lines. They are habits that keep showing up when the adversary is real, the data is messy, and the model has to earn trust in production.

Invent for the limit

Off-the-shelf stacks are fine when they fit. I reach for a custom design when the constraint is the point: a model that must stay tiny, scripts that are not Latin, latency that will not move, or explainability that has to be governed rather than demoed. Ravana existed because a generic OCR path would not hold. Sigma V4.5 existed because trees were following historical feature paths that modern synthetic fraud does not respect. The invention is an answer to a limit, not a preference for novelty.

Stay for the whole path

I care about the data, the training loop, the evaluation bar, the explainability surface, and the serving path. A notebook that never reaches a decision is unfinished work for me. The hard part is often the migration: what the old stack still catches, what the new one changes, and how you measure that without fooling yourself. I would rather own that awkward middle than hand off a model that looks good in isolation.

Put the break next to the uplift

Leakage, label noise, null patterns that hide fraud, migration risk. I put those next to the uplift instead of burying them. In DocV work, cleanup was not prep: it changed what we were willing to ship. I would rather lose a clean slide than ship a story that collapses under a second look.

Prefer work that outlives the project

Shared libraries, APIs, and pipelines matter more to me than a model that wins once. The early EmailAuthScore path and Scala decisioning work are still useful long after the original team shape changed. I frame problems, set evaluation bars, and work closely with peers, product, and platform. I do not manage people. Technical leadership for me means steering the cycle, not owning a reporting line.

The work

Four problems I lived inside long enough to feel the constraint in my hands. Each one forced an invention or a foundation, then a path into something production could trust. Metrics below are cleared; team and company outcomes are framed as such.

Sigma V4.5

Tabular transformers for fraud

Tree models follow historical feature paths. A lot of modern identity and synthetic fraud wants joint structure across features, plus explanations you can actually govern once the model is in production. Fine-tuning a familiar stack would not change the inductive bias that was the problem.

I built a transformer stack for Sigma from scratch: features as tokens, the training pipeline, and production explainability. I guided a small experimentation cycle until the approach was stable enough to hand into production work. Other teams kept going after that handoff. The architecture choice was deliberate: treat tabular fraud more like a structured sequence problem, then make the explanations hold up under the same governance bar as the score.

Earlier DocV work changed how I thought about tabular fraud. Cross-domain transfer is not a slogan when the adversary is the same. Checking what the old stack still caught mattered as much as the early lift. A transformer that wins an offline eval and then fails the migration conversation is not finished.

On an early ~422K-transaction evaluation against production Sigma V4: roughly +4% relative PR-AUC, and about +2.3 percentage points fraud capture at 5% risk depth. Explainability quality checks: 8/8. About 54 ms P50 latency on a 429-feature run.

DocV Omega

Document fraud when the features were a mess

Static document checks lose to synthetics, deepfakes, and injection attacks. On the modeling side we had roughly 2500 noisy signals that were hard to explain, hard to stabilize, and easy to overfit. The product still needed a large intelligence space. What it did not need was a core nobody could trust.

I cut that feature mess down to about 200 explainable features, with AI-agent help on the rescue work, then rebuilt the modeling and explainability pipeline around that core. I coordinated with product, engineering, and platform while keeping the focus on a foundation you could govern. The public product still talks about a large variable space. My piece was making the modeling core something you could defend.

Cleanup is not prep work. Label noise and leakage, including fraud that hid in null patterns, changed what we were willing to ship. The big capture numbers are team and product outcomes. My contribution was the feature foundation, the pipeline, and the rigor that made those outcomes possible to pursue honestly.

Public Socure product claims for DocV Omega: 98% fraud capture in the riskiest 5%, false-reject under 1%. One team milestone on the Predictive DocV path: detection moved from 43.9% to 96.9% at 97.1% precision on about 248K transactions. Those are program results; attribute them that way.

Ravana

A small KVP transformer for any Unicode

International identity documents, non-Latin scripts, very little labeled data, and a model that had to stay tiny enough to ship. A large vision-language stack was not an option for the window we had. The off-the-shelf path would not hold the constraint set.

I designed Ravana from scratch: Unicode-byte embeddings, geometry, pointer-style decoding, and synthetic training from few samples. About 2.8M parameters, roughly 11 MB, about 0.3 seconds on GPU. Field accuracies reached up to about 99% by document type in evaluation. There is a related USPTO publication on synthetic-training key-value pair extraction.

It only ran for a short window on Latin American and Chinese documents before vision-language models took over that lane. I keep it in the story anyway. It is still the clearest time I invented an architecture because the constraint demanded it, not because inventing felt impressive.

Published as US20260134337A1. Model size, latency, and field accuracy figures above are from the cleared personal contribution bank.

ID+ foundations

EmailAuthScore and early decisioning

Early Socure needed identity scoring and a decisioning surface that could absorb vendors, rules, and later machine learning without constant rewrites. The platform had to outlive the first model and the first team shape.

Founding-era engineering on and for Socure: AuthScore into the EmailAuthScore API path, Scala libraries, and configurable decision services. That evaluate surface still carries the legacy EmailAuthScore name for compatibility. A lot of later DocV and fraud work sits on the habit this period taught me: build the primitive so the next project does not have to rebuild the floor.

Systems that stay useful after the original project is gone are the thread that runs through documents, fraud models, and the agentic data-science work I am on now. Invention gets attention. Foundations keep paying rent.
